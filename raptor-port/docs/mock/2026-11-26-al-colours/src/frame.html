Telling one amendment from another

What this says in plain words:

  • The new set keeps clear of the schedule’s own colours, and pays for it. Among themselves the new seven are a little harder to tell apart than today’s (the “all seven” row), because the strong colours that were left are already a section’s. The first two, lemon and pink, are far apart for everyone.
  • For a red-green colour-blind reader, no set of seven works. Their eyes sort colours along one line, from yellow to blue. Today AL2 and AL3 look the same to them. In the new set AL1 and AL2 stay clearly apart; AL2 pink and AL3 ice come close; the later ones run together. My first page’s set was better on this one point (its first three stayed apart) and worse on the one you raised.
  • So colour is a help, never the only sign. That is why my recommendation keeps the line under changed words and one “Changed in” line per day, which names each amendment once. The order still matters: the two safest colours go to AL1 and AL2, which a day reaches most often.

The four questions

Question 1. Changed times, names, areas and remarks: colour instead of the “AL1” words?

When it happens
Every time anyone opens a published day that has since been amended. A member on his phone checking what moved since he last looked; a scheduler checking his own amendment after it goes out. Daily, for everyone.
Example
Monday 13 Jul. AL1 moved SODB to 18:00–20:00. AL2 renamed the MET brief. AL3 moved WPNS & TACTICS SYNC to 17:00–20:00. AL4 moves OCU PROGRESS REVIEW to 15:00 and is still waiting. Today each of those wears a coloured line and a tiny “AL1”, “AL2”, “AL3” under or beside it. See pictures 1a to 3c, or the try-out.
Choices and their costs

A. Today. A line and the small “ALn” words. Costs: every changed time grows its row by a line; the words are about 7 points tall and hard to read on a phone; on the Scheduler Board a whole box is outlined, and an AL1 box looks exactly like the box you are typing in.

B. Colour only. The changed words simply turn the amendment’s colour. Cleanest. Costs: a change still waiting looks the same as one already out; a red-green colour-blind reader, and anyone reading a black-and-white print, sees no mark at all; you must remember which colour is which amendment; and with today’s colours AL4 is white, the same as unchanged words, so it vanishes (picture 2b: 15:00 and INTEL + THREAT UPDATE).

C. Colour, a thin line under the words, and one “Changed in” line per day. The words take the colour, a thin line stays under them (solid once out, dotted while waiting), the small “ALn” words go, and each amended day gets one line under its signed line: “Changed in AL1 AL2 AL3 AL4 waiting”, each drawn in its colour. Costs: that one extra line on an amended day, about the height of the signed line; the number is no longer beside each change (on a desktop, resting the pointer on a change still says “Changed at AL1”).

Recommendation
C. It gives you what you asked for, the words in colour and no “AL” clutter, and the line under the words keeps three things colour alone would lose: waiting against out, the colour-blind reader, and a black-and-white print. Rows get shorter, because the tag no longer needs a line of its own under a time.
What it changes of your earlier decisions
You decided that times, areas and remarks keep their marks (the line, the box and the small ALn words) when the puck’s ring was taken off. They would keep a mark, but lose the words and, on the Scheduler Board, the box. The fix that put the “AL1” under a time on a phone, because it was cut off beside it, would no longer be needed. A waiting input’s dotted name on the Unavailable list stays dotted and also takes the colour.

Question 2. A changed puck: recolour it?

When it happens
Whenever an amendment swaps a person: the most common amendment there is.
Example
AL2 put Hunter on WPNS & TACTICS SYNC in place of Trident. Today Hunter’s puck wears a small “AL2” chip on its corner.
Why not the puck itself

A puck’s colours are already full. Olive means pilot, dark green WSO, white ground crew; the small box means his CAT; a red, amber or grey edge is a warning; purple is “this is you” or SANS. Paint the puck in the amendment’s colour and one of those is gone:

Choices and their costs

A. Today’s chip, “AL4” on the corner. Cost: the words are tiny and it covers the top of the CAT box.

B. The callsign in the amendment’s colour. Cost: coloured letters on olive or green are hard to read, and on a white ground-crew puck most of them cannot be read at all (figures below); the callsign is the one thing on a puck that must always be readable.

C. A small tab on the corner, no words. Today’s chip with its words taken out and about a third of the size, in the amendment’s colour: filled once out, an empty ring while waiting. Cost: no number on the puck; the day’s “Changed in” line gives it.

D. The chip with only its number, “4” instead of “AL4”. Cost: still a tiny number to read, and numbers stay on pucks when they have gone from everything else.

E. Nothing on the puck. Cost: a swapped person, the change people most need to see, would be the one change with no mark.

Recommendation
C, the small tab. It keeps your rule that a changed puck wears a tag and never a ring, keeps filled for out and hollow for waiting, and leaves every colour on the puck meaning what it means now. Try all five under “A changed puck wears”.
What it changes of your earlier decisions
Nothing in substance: a changed puck is still marked by its tag alone, filled once out, hollow while waiting, never a ring. The tag loses its words and its dots (at that size a plain ring reads better than a dotted one). The marks planned for a changed ALL AVAIL crowd would use the same tab.

Question 3. Which seven colours?

When it happens
On every amended day. A colour is picked once and then seen daily by everyone.
Example
On the Scheduler Board today an AL1 time box is outlined in the app’s own cyan, so it looks like the box you are typing in. AL4 is white, the colour of every unchanged word. AL2’s yellow sits close to the amber of an advisory, AL3’s green close to the green that means fine.
Choices and their costs

A. Keep today’s seven. Cost: the clashes in the table above, and colour-only words are not possible while AL4 is white.

B. The new seven: AL1 lemon, AL2 pink, AL3 ice, AL4 clay, AL5 sky, AL6 sage, AL7 and later lilac. None sits on a colour the schedule already uses for a section, a puck or a warning; all are bright enough for small letters on every panel I measured. Costs: people who know today’s colours relearn them once; from AL3 on they are pale tints, quieter than today’s and closer to each other, so the day’s “Changed in” line matters more; a red-green colour-blind reader can rely on colour for AL1 and AL2 only; and AL2 is pink (see look-alikes).

C. Your own mix. Tap any AL swatch in the try-out and pick from the short list.

Recommendation
B. If you would rather keep AL1 cyan because people know it, the cost is that an AL1 mark and a pressable or focused thing stay the same colour.
What it changes of your earlier decisions
It replaces the seven AL colours. You said “no pink” about the Leave War’s kinds of entry; if you meant the whole app, AL2 needs another colour (the choice is under look-alikes). AL8 and later would still share AL7’s colour, as you decided; without a number on each mark, two late amendments on one day could then only be told apart from the day’s “Changed in” line. The ORIG tag stays without a colour.

Question 4. Can the AL chip go completely?

When it happens
The same moments as question 1: anyone reading an amended day.
Example
Sidewinder is put on STANDARDISATION MEETING in AL4, which is still waiting. Today his puck wears a hollow dotted “AL4”.
What is lost if it goes with nothing in its place
  • Which amendment. The number is the only thing that says AL2 rather than AL3 to someone who has not learnt the colours, or cannot see them.
  • Waiting or out. Hollow against filled is the only sign that a change has not gone out yet.
  • That a puck changed at all, since a puck cannot change colour (question 2).
  • The colour-blind reader, a screen reader and a black-and-white print get nothing from colour.
What has to replace it
  • The puck’s small tab (question 2) and the thin line under changed words (question 1): these keep “changed” and “waiting or out” without any words.
  • One “Changed in” line per amended day: this keeps the number, once, instead of on every mark.
  • The day’s own tag at the top, “AL3”, stays as it is. It is the day’s version, not a mark on a change.
  • The words a screen reader speaks, and the words shown when the pointer rests on a change (“Changed at AL1”, “Edited, goes out as AL4”), stay. On the week’s changed words they would need adding; the board’s boxes and the pucks already have them.
  • A printed or exported schedule keeps today’s small “ALn” words, because paper is often black and white.
Recommendation
Take the words off; keep the mark. Removing the chip completely, with colour as the only sign, is not safe: it fails for a waiting change, for a puck, and for anyone who cannot see the colours.
What it changes of your earlier decisions
The same two as questions 1 and 2. Nothing changes about what counts as an amendment, how days are numbered, or the ORIG tag.

Not checked: how many amendments a real day reaches (the demo week has none until I made these four, so “a day rarely passes AL3” is my assumption); whether your ground crew and SANS people are screened for colour vision as aircrew are; a real iPhone (the phone pictures are Safari’s engine at your iPhone’s size on the PC, and the gaps under the lines were measured there, not on the phone itself); the colours on a printed page; AL5, AL6 and AL7 on the real screen (the staged day stops at AL4, so those three are judged from the figures and the swatches); whether “no pink” was meant for the whole app.
